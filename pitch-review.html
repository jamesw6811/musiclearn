1. Hearing inside a note

Distinguish the pitch you follow from the changing sound around it. · About 14 minutes

A voice with two lines

Welcome to The Inside of a Note. This is a course about listening to pitch and tone color. Over five sessions, we'll hear how much can happen within a held note, how musicians tune notes together, and how music can move beyond the familiar pitches of a piano.

Each recording gets a short introduction of its own. I'll explain one idea and suggest something to listen for; then you'll have time with the music. Everything plays in sequence. You can leave the screen alone, and you don't need to read music or recognize a particular interval. Some recordings are excerpts, so they'll finish before the complete piece does.

Today we'll start with sounds that let us hear inside a note. Anna-Maria Hefele's overtone-singing demonstration is our first example. Her voice has a lower line and a much higher, whistle-like line. Changing the shape of her mouth and throat brings particular components of her voice forward.

Those components are called harmonics. Frequency is the speed of a vibration; faster vibration generally gives us higher pitch. In a harmonic tone, they vibrate at whole-number multiples of a basic frequency: twice as fast, three times as fast, and so on. Usually they blend into the sound of one voice. Here, some become easy to follow separately.

As the demonstration plays, settle your attention on the lower voice. Then listen above it for the whistle-like line. Notice when one line moves while the other stays in place.

Recording reference

Changing the color of a bass line

Hefele brought different harmonics forward by changing the shape of her vocal tract. In Phuture's “Acid Tracks,” an electronic filter changes the balance of frequencies in a synthesizer sound.

The bass figure repeats, giving you time to get used to its pattern. Its tone can become subdued, bright, or sharply nasal. Musicians call this quality of a sound its timbre. Timbre helps us recognize a voice or an instrument even when it plays the same pitch as something else.

A low-pass filter reduces the higher frequencies. Moving its cutoff changes how much of that upper sound gets through. Resonance can emphasize frequencies near the cutoff, making the change more pronounced.

We'll hear five minutes. Follow the repeated bass figure and listen to its changing brightness. The notes also move, so let the repetition help you distinguish a change of pitch from a change of timbre.

Playing the harmonics separately

We've heard harmonics brought forward within a voice and within a synthesizer tone. A brass player can also use the harmonic series as a set of separate notes.

The opening of Benjamin Britten's Serenade for Tenor, Horn and Strings is a short horn solo. Britten asks for natural harmonics. The player changes between resonances of the instrument without using the valves to supply the usual range of notes.

Some of those natural notes differ from nearby piano pitches. The seventh and eleventh harmonics are particularly noticeable examples. You don't need to count them as they pass. Listen to the shape of the melody and notice any note whose placement feels unfamiliar. That placement is part of the written music.

Barry Tuckwell plays this opening solo. Follow each note as a separate step, with the sound of the horn staying recognizable throughout.

2. Hearing against a drone

Hear a stable reference beneath a moving line, across different musical settings. · About 20 minutes

Getting to know the background

In the first session, we listened inside individual sounds. Today we'll listen to a melody in relation to a sound that continues beneath it. A sustained or repeatedly renewed background like this is called a drone.

We'll begin with two minutes of tanpura on its own, played by Saiful Taankar. The tanpura is a plucked string instrument used to accompany Indian classical music. Its repeating strings provide a tonal reference for the performer. The sound also has a bright, buzzing quality shaped by the contact between string and bridge.

Let your ear get used to the repeating pattern. After a pluck, notice how the upper sound changes while the string continues ringing. This is the kind of background we'll hear beneath a singer in the next recording. For now, stay with the tanpura itself and the overlapping decay of its strings.

A singer approaches a note

Now we'll hear Pandit Pran Nath in the opening of Raga Darbari. The tanpura continues underneath him, giving his moving voice a stable reference.

A raga includes characteristic ways of moving among notes. The approach to a pitch, the time spent there, and the movement away from it all contribute to the phrase. This opening gives those movements space.

You may hear a note as settled in relation to the drone, then feel that relationship change as the voice moves. That experience doesn't require you to name the notes. It also doesn't mean that every settled moment has the same mathematical explanation; tone color, phrasing, and tuning all affect what you hear.

We'll hear the first five minutes. Follow one sustained vocal phrase from its approach through its resting point. Keep a little attention on the tanpura underneath, so you can hear the voice in relation to it.

A steady center in a song

The Beatles' “Tomorrow Never Knows” gives us another setting for a persistent tonal center. The repeating drum pattern and the voice keep the song moving, while sounds enter and leave around them.

Following an improvisation over a repeated foundation

In Alice Coltrane's “Journey in Satchidananda,” the background has several layers. A drone continues underneath a repeating bass figure, while the instruments above it have room to develop their phrases.

3. Tuning notes together

Understand what singers can adjust and why a fixed keyboard needs a tuning system. · About 16 minutes

A quartet holds a chord

Today we'll listen to notes sounding together. So far, we've followed harmonics within a note and melodies against drones. Both will help us understand tuning: choosing the pitch of one note in relation to another.

We'll start with Vocal Spectrum singing “Go the Distance.” Four singers can adjust their pitches while they listen to the chord they are making. Their vowels and timing also help the voices blend.

Barbershop singers describe a well-blended, resonant chord as ringing. Harmonics from different voices can reinforce one another and contribute to that impression. Hearing a ringing chord doesn't tell us its exact tuning, but it gives us a reason to pay attention to the sound between the individual parts.

Listen through the song to the sustained ending. As the chord is held, let your attention move from the lead voice to the combined sound of all four singers.

Recording reference

What a keyboard has to decide in advance

A singer can adjust a note as the chord changes. On a conventionally tuned keyboard, the same key keeps its assigned pitch across many different chords. That creates a practical tuning problem.

An octave is the distance between a note and the next higher or lower version of the same note name, such as C to C. Its frequency ratio is two to one. A pure fifth has a ratio of three to two, and a pure major third has a ratio of five to four. A keyboard with twelve fixed pitches per octave cannot make all those relationships pure in every key. Tuning systems distribute the differences in different ways.

Twelve-tone equal temperament makes all twelve steps the same size. Other temperaments make them unequal, so the intervals vary more from key to key.

This comparison plays Bach's Prelude in C twice within one recording: first in equal temperament, then in Werckmeister Three. The performance repeats, so the restart marks the change. Listen to the resonance within the broken chords. The difference may be subtle; you don't need to decide that one version is better.

Giving a chord time

Bach's prelude moves through its chords fairly quickly. Kali Malone's “Spectacle of Ritual” gives us more time to stay with a sustained organ sound.

When nearby frequencies sound together, they can reinforce and partly cancel each other in a repeating pattern. We hear that as beating: a pulse within a sound. Harmonics belonging to different notes can interact this way too. The rate of the pulse depends on the difference between the frequencies involved.

A held chord can therefore contain movement even when the player keeps the same notes down. The instrument, tuning, and room all contribute to what reaches us.

We'll hear five minutes. Choose one sustained sound and listen within it for a pulse or shimmer. If you hear one, stay with its speed for a little while. If it is hard to separate, notice how the whole texture changes when the next note enters.

4. Moving between pitches

Distinguish expressive pitch movement from choosing a different set of notes. · About 20 minutes

The shape of a sung note

Our last session concerned pitches chosen to sound together. Today we'll follow pitches as they move, beginning with the voice and guitar in Robert Johnson's “Cross Road Blues.” Later, we'll hear instruments built or programmed to offer a different choice of notes.

In blues, a sung pitch can bend and shade its way through a phrase. The term blue note describes characteristic pitch inflections, but it doesn't give every singer a single fixed frequency to aim for. A pitch can draw some of its expression from its changing relationship to the accompaniment.

You can hear this without translating it into piano keys. Follow the shape of Johnson's voice as he draws out a syllable. Notice how it approaches a pitch and how it leaves. Let the guitar give you a second line to hear against the voice.

Recording reference

Arriving with a slide

Duane Allman's slide guitar in the Allman Brothers Band's “Statesboro Blues” lets us follow a similar kind of movement on a string instrument.

A slide changes the vibrating length of the string as it moves. It allows a continuous path through pitches between the usual fret positions. Once the player reaches a note, a small movement back and forth can produce vibrato, an oscillation around the pitch.

The approach and the arrival give you two things to hear within a single gesture. A long slide can make you anticipate its destination; a brief one can become part of the note's beginning.

We'll hear four minutes of the Fillmore East recording. Follow the slide guitar into one held note, then listen to what happens after it arrives. Notice whether the pitch rests, wavers, or continues into another phrase.

Recording reference

Adding places to stop

A slide travels continuously through pitch. A fretted instrument gives the player repeatable places to stop the string. Adding frets can make additional pitches available in an ordinary fingering pattern.

King Gizzard and the Lizard Wizard use modified instruments in “Rattlesnake.” The repeating riff gives us time to learn the placement of its notes, including pitches outside the usual twelve-note keyboard system.

Microtonal is a broad term for music using such pitch distinctions or other tuning systems. It doesn't prescribe one scale, and adding extra frets doesn't mean every available pitch has to appear in a song.

We'll hear six minutes. Follow the main riff through its returns. As it becomes familiar, listen for the particular spacing between its notes. You can learn the shape of a phrase before you have names for its intervals.

Recording reference

An electronic track with twenty-two steps

In Sevish's “Gleam,” the pitch system divides the octave into twenty-two equal steps. This is another way to build a repeatable set of notes.

Equal temperament describes equal spacing within the octave; twelve is one possible number of divisions. Twenty-two offers a different collection of intervals, so familiar ideas such as a repeating melody or a chord progression can take on different pitch relationships.

You don't need to count the steps. The electronic beat and returning phrases give you ways to follow the track while its intervals become familiar.

For this four-minute excerpt, choose a melodic phrase and listen for its return. Notice which part of its shape you recognize each time, even if you wouldn't know where to play it on a piano.

5. Composing with the sound itself

Bring pitch, tuning, and timbre together in extended musical examples. · About 24 minutes

A piano tuned for its resonances

In our final session, we'll use the listening habits we've developed across the course. We'll begin with Michael Harrison's “Tone Cloud One,” from Revelation: Music in Pure Intonation.

Harrison uses a specially tuned piano. Just intonation describes tunings built from whole-number frequency ratios, including relationships like the pure fifth we discussed in the keyboard session. A composer can choose those relationships for a particular work rather than distribute the same compromise across every key.

That choice doesn't mean every combination of notes will be free of beating. The relationships among all their harmonics can be complex. Harrison's repeated figures give us time to hear the piano's resonance accumulating around the individual strikes.

We'll let this piece run for a little over eight minutes. Begin by following the struck notes. As they overlap, turn your attention toward the sustained sound around them and any higher tones you can pick out within it.

An ensemble combines into a sound

The piano gave us many overlapping resonances from one instrument. In the opening of Gérard Grisey's Partiels, several instruments combine into a sound whose inner components change over time.

Spectral music takes the frequency content of sound as material for composition. An ensemble can distribute components across instruments and change their relative strength, their timing, and their tuning. That brings us back to the harmonics we heard Anna-Maria Hefele bringing forward in the first session.

You may sometimes hear individual instruments and sometimes hear a more unified sound. Both ways of listening are useful here.

We'll hear the first five minutes. Begin with the low recurring sound. As the ensemble grows above it, follow the changing upper texture and notice when an individual instrumental voice becomes distinct.

Small movements within a repeated phrase

Boards of Canada's “Roygbiv” brings us back to a compact electronic track. We can listen to its repeating parts with the same attention we've given to longer, sustained sounds.

A synthesizer tone can have movement within it even when the melodic pattern repeats. Nearby components may beat against each other; the pitch of a whole tone may drift; its brightness may change. Those effects can overlap. The recording alone doesn't establish the equipment or process behind every detail.

Let the repeated phrase become familiar, then attend to its sound on each return. In this short track, listen for small changes in the steadiness of the pitch. Notice whether you hear a line moving as a whole or a less definite shimmer within it.

Hearing a voice within a chord

We'll finish with Imogen Heap's “Hide and Seek.” It returns us to a voice, now heard in a texture of vocal pitches moving together.

Listen to the consonants at the beginnings and ends of words. When several parts share that timing, they can seem closely joined even as they occupy different pitches. A harmony processor can derive additional pitches from a vocal performance while retaining much of its articulation.

This gives us a useful comparison with the overtone singing at the beginning of the course. There, the upper line brought out harmonics within a voice. Here, attend to the separate notes of the chord and the way their syllables move together.

Let the words guide you through the song. On a held vowel, widen your attention to the chord around the melody. Then hear how the next consonant gathers the parts into a shared gesture.
